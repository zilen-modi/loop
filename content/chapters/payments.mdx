Payments are where a system design meets money, and the interviewer wants three guarantees: **charge once**, **record everything**, and **find out when you're wrong**. None of them needs exotic technology. Each needs one design decision made deliberately.

## Requirements and numbers

- Charge a customer through an outside payment provider; show the payment's status.
- Record every movement of money: customers, Tiffin, restaurants, riders.
- Match our records against the provider's daily settlement file.

Peak: 800 payments a second. About 5% of requests are retried after a timeout. The provider takes about 800 ms per charge and sometimes times out itself.

## Charge once: idempotency keys

The app can't tell "the request was lost" from "the response was lost", so it retries, and retries are where double charges come from.

<Checkpoint id="pay-retries" />

The fix is an <Term id="idempotency-key">idempotency key</Term>: the app generates a unique key for each payment attempt and sends it with every retry. The server writes the payment intent **and the key in one transaction**, under a unique constraint, before calling the provider. A retry with the same key finds the existing payment and gets its result back instead of charging again. Pass the same key on to the provider, which supports it for the same reason.

Where the key lives matters. A key in a cache can be evicted or lost with a node, and then the next retry charges again. A key that can vanish isn't a guarantee.

## Record everything: double entry

Accountants solved this centuries ago. Every movement of money is written as entries that **sum to zero**: the customer's account goes down by ₹500 and Tiffin's holding account goes up by ₹500. Balances aren't stored; they're the sum of an account's entries.

The ledger is append-only. A refund is new entries in the other direction, never an edit. Concurrent writers can't lose each other's updates, because nobody overwrites anything, and a bug shows up as books that don't add up to zero.

## The flow, step by step

1. **Intent:** insert the payment (status `pending`) with the idempotency key. A duplicate key returns the existing payment.
2. **Charge:** call the provider with a timeout.
3. **Outcome:** in one transaction, set the status and write the ledger entries. If the provider timed out, set `unknown` and don't guess.
4. **Status:** the app polls; serve those reads from a replica.

## Find out when you're wrong: reconciliation

Every day the provider sends a settlement file: what it actually charged and paid out. A job reads it from object storage, reads the ledger from a replica, and matches them payment by payment. `unknown` payments are resolved; anything charged but not recorded (or recorded but not charged) is flagged for a person. Reconciliation is how a payment system finds the errors no single request can see.

## In interviews

What you'll usually be asked next:

- "Refunds." New ledger entries in the reverse direction, with their own idempotency key.
- "Webhooks from the provider." Verify the signature, handle each one idempotently, and expect them late, twice or out of order.
- "Currency." Integers in the smallest unit (paise), with the currency code; never floating point.
- "A second provider." Route by method or cost, fail over on outages; reconciliation runs per provider.

Traps that cost people the round:

- Retrying the provider without an idempotency key.
- A balance column updated in place.
- Floats for money.
- Assuming a timeout means the charge failed.
