"Design an elevator system" is a favourite because the classes are obvious (Building, Elevator, Request) but the behaviour isn't. Two policies decide whether the design is any good: where each car goes next, and which car answers a call. Get those exact, keep them separate, and the rest is bookkeeping.

## Two kinds of request

- **Hall calls:** someone on a floor presses Up or Down. Any car can answer, so the controller must choose one.
- **Car calls:** someone inside presses a floor. Only that car can serve it.

Keep them apart in your model. Car calls become stops for that car; hall calls are assigned to a car (becoming one of its stops) but are also remembered as hall calls, so they can be reassigned if the car breaks down, and timed.

## Where does a car go next? SCAN

Going to the nearest stop first sounds efficient and is how the story's first car behaves. It zigzags, and far floors can wait forever. The classic answer is <Term id="scan">SCAN</Term>: keep going in your direction while any stop lies ahead; when none does, turn around.

SCAN is fair: nobody waits more than about two sweeps of the building. It's the same algorithm operating systems once used to move a disk's read head, which is why it's also called the elevator algorithm.

## Which car answers? A cost function

Give each car a cost for the call and pick the cheapest (ties to the lowest number, so tests are deterministic):

- An idle car, or one already **heading that way in the same direction and not yet past the floor**, costs the distance.
- Any other car must first finish its sweep: the distance to its furthest stop ahead, plus the distance back to the caller.

<Checkpoint id="el-cost" />

Putting this rule in one function is the design decision interviewers look for. "Nearest car" is a one-line change; so is weighing load or energy later.

## Time in ticks

Real controllers are driven by events, but for a build and its tests, **ticks** are better: in one tick a car moves one floor or stops at a floor it was asked to. Every car's position is then exact at every step, and a whole morning rush hour can be replayed deterministically.

## The extension

**Out of service.** A car is taken out: it gets no new hall calls, and the ones it was carrying are reassigned to other cars by the same cost rule. Passengers inside still reach their floors. This is easy only if hall calls were tracked separately from stops.

**Wait times.** Each hall call remembers when it was made; when its car stops at that floor, record the wait. That turns the building into something you can measure, and compare policies with.

## In interviews

What you'll usually be asked next:

- "Destination dispatch." Passengers enter their floor in the lobby, and the system groups people going to nearby floors into the same car.
- "Capacity." A full car skips hall calls until someone gets off.
- "Peak hours." Park idle cars at the lobby in the morning and spread them out in the evening.
- "Minimise average wait, not distance." Change the cost function; nothing else.

Traps that cost people the round:

- Nearest-stop-first movement, which zigzags and starves far floors.
- Treating every request the same, so hall calls can't be reassigned.
- Dispatch logic scattered through the movement code.
- Real timers and threads in a 90-minute build.
